# Glossary review

137 terms, in the order definitions build up.

1. **Strings and string numbers** — The guitar has six strings, numbered from the thinnest: string 1 is the thin, highest-sounding high E (nearest the floor as you hold it) and string 6 is the thick, low E. Diagrams draw them as vertical lines with string 6 on the left.
   *Why it works:* Thinner, tighter strings vibrate faster and so sound higher, which is why the numbering runs from thin and high to thick and low.
   _match: string, strings, string 1, string 2, string 3, string 4, string 5, string 6, low e, high e_

2. **Fret** — The thin metal strips across the long arm of the guitar. Pressing a string just behind a fret shortens the part that vibrates, so it sounds higher; "fret 3" means the space just behind strip number 3, counting from the top.
   *Why it works:* A shorter string vibrates faster; each fret raises the sound by the same small step, and 12 frets up gives the same sound one level higher.
   _match: fret, frets, fretted, fretting_

3. **Fretboard / neck** — The neck is the long wooden arm of the guitar; its playing surface, with the frets set into it, is the fretboard. The dots on it mark frets 3, 5, 7, 9 and 12 to help you find your place.
   *Why it works:* Every sound the guitar can make sits somewhere on this grid of strings and frets, so learning its layout is learning where sounds live.
   _match: fretboard, fretboards, neck_

4. **Nut** — The small slotted strip at the top of the neck, where it meets the headstock. Strings rest in its slots, and a string played without fretting vibrates from the nut down to where it is anchored on the body; diagrams show it as a thick top line.
   *Why it works:* The nut sets the full length of each string, so it fixes the lowest sound a string can make; every fret counts up from it.
   _match: nut_

5. **Bridge (two meanings)** — 1) On the guitar, the bridge is the part on the body where the strings are anchored. 2) In a song, the bridge is a contrasting section, usually heard once, that takes the music somewhere new before the familiar parts come back.
   *Why it works:* Both meanings are links: one carries the strings' vibration into the body, the other gives the ear a contrast so the return of the familiar sounds fresh.
   _match: bridge, bridges_

6. **Chord diagram / chord box** — A grid picture of the neck as if the guitar stood upright facing you: vertical lines are strings (string 6 on the left), horizontal lines are frets, dots show where fingers go. In this app, "the card" is the panel under the practice steps; besides finger diagrams it can show a fretboard map or a picking pattern.
   *Why it works:* It turns where your fingers go into a picture you read at a glance, so you can spend your attention on how it sounds.
   _match: chord diagram, chord diagrams, chord box, chord boxes, card_

7. **Tab** — Guitar writing with six horizontal lines, one per string; the top line is string 1 (high E) and the bottom is string 6. A number on a line tells you which fret to play on that string; 0 means play it without fretting.
   *Why it works:* Tab shows exactly where to play on the fretboard, which makes it the quickest way to read a part on guitar.
   _match: tab, tabs, tablature_

8. **Fretting hand / picking hand** — The fretting hand presses strings against the fretboard (the left hand for most right-handed players). The picking hand sounds the strings over the guitar's body, with fingers or a plastic tool.
   *Why it works:* Two jobs, two hands: the fretting hand chooses how high each string sounds, the picking hand decides when and how loudly it sounds.
   _match: fretting hand, fretting-hand, fretting hands, picking hand, pick hand, picking-hand_

9. **Pick** — A small, thin piece of plastic held between thumb and first finger of the picking hand and used to strike the strings. Also called a plectrum; to "pick" a string is to sound it this way.
   *Why it works:* A pick gives a clear, even attack on each string, which helps you sound strings exactly when you mean to.
   _match: pick, picks, plectrum_

10. **Strum** — Brushing the pick or fingers across several strings in one sweeping movement, downwards (towards the floor) or upwards, so the strings sound together.
   *Why it works:* Strumming sounds several strings at once and keeps time, so it is how a guitar fills out a song and drives it forward.
   _match: strum, strums, strummed, strumming_

11. **Mute / muted** — Stopping strings from ringing by touching them lightly, with either hand, so a strum gives a short thud or "chk" instead of a ringing sound. A muted strum still moves in time; it just sounds dull.
   *Why it works:* Silence is part of rhythm: cutting the ring short lets you place sounds and gaps exactly where you want them.
   _match: mute, mutes, muted, muting_

12. **Shape** — A fixed pattern of fingers on the fretboard that you can learn once and recognise again. Many shapes can be moved up or down the neck as a block and still work.
   *Why it works:* Because every fret is the same size step, moving a whole shape along the neck keeps the gaps between its sounds the same, just higher or lower.
   _match: shape, shapes_

13. **Position** — Where your fretting hand sits along the neck, named by the fret your first finger covers. With your first finger at fret 5, the other fingers cover frets 6, 7 and 8, one finger per fret.
   *Why it works:* Staying in one position lets you reach a whole group of sounds without moving your hand, so you can listen rather than hunt.
   _match: position, positions_

14. **Note** — A single musical sound at one particular height (how high or low it sounds). Notes are named with the letters A to G; each string and fret on the guitar gives you one.
   *Why it works:* A note is one steady vibration; music is built by choosing which of these vibrations to play together or one after another.
   _match: note, notes_

15. **Pitch** — How high or low a note sounds. It is set by how many times per second the string vibrates (its frequency, in hertz): thinner, tighter or shorter strings vibrate faster and sound higher.
   *Why it works:* Pitch is frequency; when two pitches' frequencies form simple ratios, their vibrations line up and the ear hears them blend.
   _match: pitch, pitches_

16. **In tune** — A note is in tune when its pitch is exactly where it should be, either matching a tuner or matching another note. Out of tune notes sound slightly sour, and you hear a slow wobble between them.
   *Why it works:* Two slightly different frequencies drift in and out of step, causing that wobble; when the pitches match exactly, the wobble stops.
   _match: in tune, in-tune, out of tune, tuning, tuned_

17. **Unison** — Two notes at exactly the same pitch, played together. On guitar, string 6 at fret 5 and string 5 played without fretting are a unison: both are A.
   *Why it works:* A unison is the simplest ratio of all, 1:1, so the two vibrations match completely and fuse into one sound.
   _match: unison, unisons_

18. **Octave** — The distance from one note to the next note with the same letter name, higher or lower. On one string it is 12 frets up: string 6 played without fretting is E, and at fret 12 it is E again, higher.
   *Why it works:* The higher note vibrates exactly twice as fast (2:1), so the two blend so closely they sound like the same note, which is why they share a name.
   _match: octave, octaves_

19. **Consonance / dissonance** — Consonant notes sound smooth and settled together; dissonant notes clash and sound rough or restless. Both are useful: music moves by building up clashes and then easing them.
   *Why it works:* Every note contains a series of higher overtones (the harmonic series); simple ratios like the octave's 2:1 or 3:2 line those overtones up, while complex ratios make nearby overtones rub.
   _match: consonant, consonance, dissonant, dissonance, clash, clashes, clashing_

20. **Drone** — One note held or repeated underneath while you play or sing other notes over it, like the low hum of bagpipes. A string left ringing, such as low E, works as a drone.
   *Why it works:* Against a fixed drone you can hear each new note's consonance or dissonance clearly, which trains your ear to feel the gaps between notes.
   _match: drone, drones_

21. **Half step** — The smallest gap between two notes in Western music: one fret on the guitar. Move from fret 3 to fret 4 on any string and you have moved a half step.
   *Why it works:* There are 12 half steps in an octave, all the same size, and every musical gap is counted in them.
   _match: half step, half steps, half-step, semitone, semitones_

22. **Whole step** — Two half steps together: two frets on the guitar, for example fret 3 to fret 5 on the same string.
   *Why it works:* Patterns of whole (W) and half (H) steps build every family of notes you'll learn; the brightest, most common one runs W-W-H-W-W-W-H.
   _match: whole step, whole steps, whole-step_

23. **Sharp and flat (# and b)** — A sharp (#) raises a note by one half step, one fret higher; a flat (b) lowers it by one half step, one fret lower. F# is one fret above F; Bb is one fret below B.
   *Why it works:* There are 12 half steps in an octave but only seven letters, so the five notes in between are named as sharps or flats of their neighbours.
   _match: sharps, flats, flattens, flattened, sharpened, flat 2nd, flat 3rd, flat 5th, flat 6th, flat 7th, sharp 4th, sharp 5th_

24. **Interval** — The distance between two notes, counted in half steps and named by how many letter names it spans: C up to E covers C, D, E, so it is numbered three.
   *Why it works:* Intervals are the building blocks of music; each has a frequency ratio, and that ratio decides how consonant or dissonant the two notes sound.
   _match: interval, intervals_

25. **Root** — The note a group of notes is built up from and named after. In a G shape, G is the root, and every other note is measured as an interval above it; diagrams often mark it specially.
   *Why it works:* Every interval is counted up from the root, so if you know where the root sits on string 6 or 5, you can move any shape and keep its sound.
   _match: root, roots, root note, root notes_

26. **3rd** — The interval spanning three letter names, such as C up to E. It comes in two sizes: three half steps (three frets) or four half steps (four frets).
   *Why it works:* Stacking 3rds on a root is the basic way notes are combined, and which size sits on the root decides whether the result sounds bright or dark.
   _match: 3rd, 3rds_

27. **Major 3rd** — The larger 3rd: four half steps, four frets up the same string. C to E and G to B are major 3rds.
   *Why it works:* Its frequency ratio is close to 5:4, a low overtone in the harmonic series, which gives a bright, happy sound when it sits on the root.
   _match: major 3rd, major 3rds, major third, major thirds_

28. **Minor 3rd** — The smaller 3rd: three half steps, three frets up the same string. A to C and E to G are minor 3rds; it is one fret narrower than a major 3rd.
   *Why it works:* Its ratio is close to 6:5; lowering a major 3rd by one half step to a minor 3rd turns a bright, happy sound dark and sad.
   _match: minor 3rd, minor 3rds, minor third, minor thirds_

29. **5th** — The interval spanning five letter names, normally seven half steps: G to D. On guitar, fret 3 on string 6 and fret 5 on string 5 make a 5th (G and D).
   *Why it works:* Its 3:2 ratio is the most consonant after the octave, so the 5th gives the root solid, stable support.
   _match: 5th, 5ths, fifth, fifths_

30. **4th** — The interval spanning four letter names, normally five half steps (five frets): G up to C. Most neighbouring guitar strings are a 4th apart.
   *Why it works:* Its 4:3 ratio is the most consonant interval after the octave and the 5th, so strings tuned in 4ths ring together cleanly and keep finger shapes within one hand's reach.
   _match: 4th, 4ths, fourth, fourths_

31. **6th** — The interval spanning six letter names: eight or nine half steps, as in C up to A (nine). Turn a 3rd upside down, moving its lower note up an octave, and you get a 6th.
   *Why it works:* A 6th is a 3rd flipped by an octave, so it keeps a 3rd's sweet, consonant blend while spreading the notes wider apart.
   _match: 6th, 6ths, sixth, sixths_

32. **7th** — The interval spanning seven letter names: ten or eleven half steps, just one or two frets short of the octave. C up to B is eleven; C up to Bb is ten.
   *Why it works:* Its frequency ratio is complex (about 15:8 or 16:9), so it sounds restless and seems to lean towards the octave just above.
   _match: 7th, 7ths, seventh, sevenths, b7_

33. **9th** — Counting past the octave: counting the root as 1, the octave is number 8, so the 9th is the 2nd an octave up, 14 half steps above the root. From C, the 9th is the D above the higher C.
   *Why it works:* Numbers above 8 mean "this note, placed an octave higher"; spreading a close 2nd out by an octave softens its clash into colour.
   _match: 9th, 9ths, ninth, ninths_

34. **11th** — The 4th an octave up: 17 half steps above the root. From C, count C D E F G A B C, then D (9), E (10), F (11): the 11th is the F above the higher C.
   *Why it works:* Subtract 7 from any number past the octave to find its smaller twin: 11 minus 7 is 4, so the 11th sounds like a 4th, spread wider.
   _match: 11th, 11ths, eleventh, elevenths_

35. **13th** — The 6th an octave up: 21 half steps above the root. From C, the 13th is the A above the higher C.
   *Why it works:* Thirteen minus seven is six, so the 13th sounds like a 6th placed high; past 13 the count wraps back onto the root's octaves.
   _match: 13th, 13ths, thirteenth, thirteenths_

36. **Scale** — A scale is a set of notes climbing in order from a starting note up to its octave, following a fixed pattern of half steps and whole steps. On guitar you can play one up a single string or across several strings in one position.
   *Why it works:* The pattern of steps gives each scale its sound; the most common runs W-W-H-W-W-W-H, and where the two half steps fall decides its mood.
   _match: scale, scales_

37. **Key** — A key is the set of notes a song mostly uses, taken from one scale, with one note that feels like home. A song 'in G' uses the scale that starts and ends on G, so most of its notes come from it.
   *Why it works:* Staying inside one scale's notes keeps things consonant and settled; notes from outside the key stand out because they break that pattern of steps.
   _match: key, keys_

38. **Home note (tonic)** — The home note (tonic) is the note a key or scale is named after and built from, the one that sounds finished when you stop on it. In G, it's G: string 6, fret 3.
   *Why it works:* Every other note is heard as a distance (an interval) from the home note, so landing on it makes the ear feel the music has arrived.
   _match: home note, home notes, tonic_

39. **Scale degree** — A scale degree is a note's number in its scale, counting up from the home note as 1. In a G scale, G is 1, A is 2, B is 3, up to 7, then G returns an octave higher as 1 again.
   *Why it works:* Numbers work in every key, and they name intervals: the 3rd degree sits a 3rd above the home note, the 5th a 5th above.
   _match: degree, degrees, scale degree, scale degrees_

40. **Major** — Major describes a scale, key or sound built on the step pattern W-W-H-W-W-W-H, with a major 3rd (four half steps) above the home note. It's the bright, happy-sounding one: think 'Do-Re-Mi'.
   *Why it works:* A major 3rd matches a strong overtone that every plucked string already contains (the 5th harmonic), which helps major sound bright and stable.
   _match: major_

41. **Minor** — Minor describes a scale, key or sound with a minor 3rd above the home note: three half steps instead of four. It sounds darker or sadder than major, and that one note, the 3rd, makes the difference.
   *Why it works:* Moving a single note down one half step changes the whole mood, which shows how much the 3rd above the home note shapes what we hear.
   _match: minor_

42. **Natural minor** — Natural minor is the scale with the step pattern W-H-W-W-H-W-W. Compared with major from the same note it has a flat 3rd, flat 6th and flat 7th. E natural minor uses the same notes as G major.
   *Why it works:* It's the same seven notes as a major scale started from a different home note, so the half steps land in new places and bright turns dark.
   _match: natural minor_

43. **Relative major / minor** — A relative major and minor share exactly the same notes but have different home notes. The relative minor starts on the 6th degree of the major scale: G major and E minor are relatives, both with one sharp (F#).
   *Why it works:* Because the notes match, only the home note changes the mood, so the same fretboard patterns serve both keys.
   _match: relative minor, relative major, relative minors, relative majors_

44. **Diatonic** — Diatonic means belonging to the key: using only the seven notes of its scale, with no extra sharps or flats. In G, playing only G, A, B, C, D, E and F# is diatonic; adding a Bb is not.
   *Why it works:* Diatonic notes all follow the key's step pattern, so they sit comfortably together; anything from outside stands out as colour.
   _match: diatonic_

45. **Circle of fifths** — The circle of fifths arranges all 12 keys like a clock, each a 5th above the last: C, G, D, A, E and so on. Each step clockwise adds one sharp; each step anticlockwise adds one flat.
   *Why it works:* Neighbouring keys share six of their seven notes, so the circle shows which keys sound closest; the 5th is the most consonant interval after the octave.
   _match: circle of fifths_

46. **Chromatic** — The chromatic scale is all 12 notes in a row, each a half step from the next: on one string, that's every fret in turn. 'Chromatic' notes are ones from outside the key.
   *Why it works:* Twelve half steps make an octave; every other scale is a selection from these 12, chosen by its pattern of steps.
   _match: chromatic_

47. **Pentatonic** — Pentatonic means a scale with five notes per octave ('penta' is Greek for five). The two common ones, major and minor, use the same five notes from different home notes: the G major and E minor versions share the same five notes.
   *Why it works:* Five-note scales skip the two half steps of the seven-note scale, so no two notes are a half step apart; that makes clashes rare, which is why they turn up worldwide.
   _match: pentatonic, pentatonics_

48. **Major pentatonic** — A five-note scale made from the major scale by leaving out its 4th and 7th degrees: 1, 2, 3, 5, 6. In G that's G, A, B, D, E. Bright and open, heard in country, rock and pop.
   *Why it works:* Removing the 4th and 7th removes both half steps, so no two notes clash and almost any note sounds good in the key.
   _match: major pentatonic_

49. **Minor pentatonic** — A five-note scale made from natural minor by leaving out its 2nd and 6th: 1, b3, 4, 5, b7. In E that's E, G, A, B, D, the box pattern most rock guitar solos start from.
   *Why it works:* With no half steps between its notes it can't clash, and it keeps the minor 3rd and flat 7th that give rock its gritty sound.
   _match: minor pentatonic_

50. **Blues scale** — The blues scale (often just 'blues') is the minor pentatonic plus one extra note, the flat 5th: 1, b3, 4, b5, 5, b7. In E: E, G, A, Bb, B, D. Major blues instead adds a flat 3rd to the major pentatonic.
   *Why it works:* The flat 5th squeezes in a half step between the 4th and 5th, a deliberate clash that sounds gritty and pulls towards its neighbours.
   _match: blues scale, blues scales, blues, minor blues, major blues_

51. **Blue note** — A blue note is a bent or slightly flattened version of a note, played for expression: usually somewhere around the flat 3rd, flat 5th or flat 7th. On guitar you often bend the minor 3rd a little towards the major 3rd.
   *Why it works:* Blue notes sit between the 12 notes of the usual Western system, so the ear hears them as expressive, bittersweet clashes rather than mistakes.
   _match: blue note, blue notes_

52. **Mode** — A mode is a scale you get by playing the same seven notes from a different home note. Seven starting points give seven modes. Guitarists mostly learn each as its own step formula from one root, and compare it to major or minor.
   *Why it works:* Moving the home note moves where the two half steps fall, which changes the intervals above the root and so the mood.
   _match: mode, modes, modal_

53. **Ionian** — Ionian is the mode built from the 1st degree of the major scale, so it simply is the major scale: W-W-H-W-W-W-H. Bright and happy; heard in most pop, folk and children's songs.
   *Why it works:* It's the reference point: the other modes are described as Ionian or natural minor with one or two notes moved a half step.
   _match: ionian_

54. **Dorian** — Dorian is natural minor with a raised 6th: W-H-W-W-W-H-W. It's also the major scale's notes started from the 2nd degree. Minor but cooler and brighter; heard in funk, soul, jazz and Santana-style rock.
   *Why it works:* Raising the 6th a half step lifts the darkest note of natural minor, giving a minor sound with a hopeful edge.
   _match: dorian_

55. **Phrygian** — Phrygian is natural minor with a flat 2nd: H-W-W-W-H-W-W. It's also the major scale's notes started from the 3rd degree. Dark and Spanish-sounding; heard in flamenco and heavy metal riffs.
   *Why it works:* The flat 2nd sits just a half step above the root, a tight, clashing step that gives it a dark, exotic edge.
   _match: phrygian_

56. **Lydian** — Lydian is major with a raised (sharp) 4th: W-W-W-H-W-W-H. It's also the major scale's notes started from the 4th degree. Dreamy and floating; heard in film and TV music and Joe Satriani-style rock.
   *Why it works:* The sharp 4th removes the half step between the 3rd and 4th, so nothing tugs downwards and the sound seems to hover.
   _match: lydian_

57. **Mixolydian** — Mixolydian is major with a flat 7th: W-W-H-W-W-H-W. It's also the major scale's notes started from the 5th degree. Bright but relaxed and bluesy; heard in classic rock, blues, folk and Celtic music.
   *Why it works:* Flattening the 7th removes the half step below the root, so there's less pull back home, giving a laid-back, rootsy sound.
   _match: mixolydian_

58. **Aeolian** — Aeolian is the mode name for natural minor: W-H-W-W-H-W-W. It's also the major scale's notes started from the 6th degree. Sad or serious; heard in rock ballads, pop and metal.
   *Why it works:* Against major, three notes drop a half step (the 3rd, 6th and 7th), which is why it sounds darker than the bright modes.
   _match: aeolian_

59. **Locrian** — Locrian is natural minor with a flat 2nd and a flat 5th: H-W-W-H-W-W-W. It's also the major scale's notes started from the 7th degree. Unstable and rarely used as home; heard mostly in metal.
   *Why it works:* Its 5th is flattened, so the strongest consonance above the root is missing and the home note never feels settled.
   _match: locrian_

60. **Harmonic minor** — Harmonic minor is natural minor with its 7th raised a half step, leaving a wide gap of three half steps between the 6th and 7th. Heard in classical music, neoclassical metal, and Middle Eastern and Eastern European folk.
   *Why it works:* The raised 7th sits a half step below the root, pulling strongly home, while the three-half-step gap gives its exotic flavour.
   _match: harmonic minor_

61. **Melodic minor** — Melodic minor is natural minor with both the 6th and 7th raised a half step: W-H-W-W-W-W-H, which is major with a flat 3rd. Classical music uses it going up only; jazz uses it both ways.
   *Why it works:* Raising the 6th as well smooths out the wide gap of harmonic minor while keeping the half step that pulls up to the root.
   _match: melodic minor_

62. **Phrygian dominant** — This scale is Phrygian with a major 3rd instead of a minor 3rd: 1, b2, 3, 4, 5, b6, b7. It's harmonic minor started from its 5th degree. It's the sound of flamenco, Middle Eastern music and much heavy metal.
   *Why it works:* The half step from the flat 2nd down to the root, plus the three-half-step gap up to the 3rd, gives its unmistakable exotic sound.
   _match: phrygian dominant_

63. **Lydian dominant** — This scale is Mixolydian with a sharp 4th, or Lydian with a flat 7th: 1, 2, 3, #4, 5, 6, b7. It's melodic minor started from its 4th degree. Used in jazz and fusion.
   *Why it works:* The sharp 4th adds Lydian's floating brightness to Mixolydian's relaxed flat 7th, a blend jazz players reach for as extra colour.
   _match: lydian dominant_

64. **Altered scale** — The altered scale is melodic minor started from its 7th degree: 1, b2, #2, 3, b5, #5, b7. Every note except the root, 3rd and flat 7th is moved a half step. Jazz players use it just before landing home.
   *Why it works:* Packing in clashing notes builds a strain the ear wants released, so arriving at the home sound feels stronger.
   _match: altered, altered scale_

65. **Bebop scale** — A bebop scale is a seven-note scale with one extra in-between note added, making eight. The most common is Mixolydian plus the natural 7th. Jazz players use it in long runs of quick notes.
   *Why it works:* Eight notes fit evenly into a count of four, so the extra note keeps the strongest notes of the scale falling on the strong counts.
   _match: bebop, bebop scale, bebop scales_

66. **Diminished scale** — This eight-note scale alternates half and whole steps (H-W-H-W-H-W-H-W, or starting with a whole step). Its pattern repeats every three frets, so one fingering moves up the neck. Used in jazz and metal for an eerie sound.
   *Why it works:* Its strict half-whole symmetry gives it no single home note, so it sounds restless and unsettled.
   _match: diminished scale, diminished scales_

67. **Chord** — Three or more notes sounding together, usually strummed across several strings at once. A chord takes its letter name from its root, the note it is built on.
   *Why it works:* Notes whose pitches line up in simple ratios blend smoothly, so stacking them in 3rds gives a settled, consonant sound.
   _match: chord, chords_

68. **Triad** — The simplest chord: three notes stacked in 3rds, the root, the 3rd and the 5th above it. Most open shapes on the guitar are triads with some of those notes doubled on extra strings.
   *Why it works:* The 3rd decides the mood: four frets above the root sounds major, three frets sounds minor, so moving that one note down a fret turns major into minor.
   _match: triad, triads_

69. **Diminished** — A triad with a minor 3rd and a 5th lowered by one fret (a flat 5th). It sounds tense and unsettled, and it is written with "dim" or a small circle, as in Bdim or B°.
   *Why it works:* Two minor 3rds stacked make a 5th that is a half step short, a clashing interval the ear wants to move away from.
   _match: diminished_

70. **Augmented** — A triad with a major 3rd and a 5th raised by one fret (a sharp 5th). It sounds dreamy and unstable, and it is written with "aug" or a plus sign, as in Caug or C+.
   *Why it works:* Two major 3rds stacked push the 5th a half step too wide; it still has a major 3rd, but that stretched 5th makes it sound unstable, as if waiting to move on.
   _match: augmented_

71. **Inversion** — Which note of a chord is lowest. Root position has the root lowest; first inversion puts the 3rd lowest; second inversion puts the 5th lowest. The chord keeps its name either way.
   *Why it works:* The same notes make the same chord in any order because 3rds and 5ths keep their identity across octaves; only the lowest note changes how grounded it sounds.
   _match: inversion, inversions, inverted, root position, first inversion, second inversion_

72. **Chord tone** — Any note that belongs to the chord being played: for a triad, its root, 3rd or 5th. Over a G chord those are G, B and D, wherever they sit on the fretboard.
   *Why it works:* Chord tones sound consonant against the chord because they are part of it, so landing a picked note on one sounds settled rather than clashing.
   _match: chord tone, chord tones_

73. **Seventh chord** — A triad with one more note stacked a 3rd higher: the 7th above the root. That makes four notes, written with a 7, as in G7, Gmaj7 or Gm7.
   *Why it works:* The 7th sits a half step or whole step below the root an octave up, adding a mild clash that makes the chord sound richer and keen to move.
   _match: seventh chord, seventh chords, seventh-chord_

74. **Shell voicing** — A stripped-down seventh chord using only the root, 3rd and 7th, with the 5th left out. On guitar it takes three strings, with the root on string 6 or string 5.
   *Why it works:* The 3rd says major or minor and the 7th says which kind of seventh chord it is; the 5th adds little, so dropping it keeps the sound clear.
   _match: shell, shells, shell voicing, shell voicings_

75. **Voicing** — How the notes of a chord are arranged: which strings, which octave, and which note sits on top or lowest. One chord can have many voicings up and down the neck.
   *Why it works:* A chord is named by its notes, not their order, but spacing changes the sound: wide gaps ring open and clear, close notes sound denser.
   _match: voicing, voicings_

76. **Sus chord** — Short for suspended: a chord with no 3rd, swapped for the 2nd (sus2) or the 4th (sus4). On open D, lift the finger off string 1 for Dsus2, or move it to fret 3 for Dsus4.
   *Why it works:* Without the 3rd the chord is neither major nor minor, so it sounds open and waiting, and the ear wants the 3rd back.
   _match: sus, sus2, sus4, suspended, sus chord, sus chords_

77. **Add chord** — A triad with one extra note and the 3rd kept. Add9 adds the 9th (the 2nd, an octave up); add11 adds the 11th. For Cadd9, play C and move the finger on string 2 to fret 3.
   *Why it works:* Keeping the 3rd keeps the chord clearly major or minor, while the added note sits a step away from one of its own notes, giving a soft, shimmering clash.
   _match: add9, add11, add chord, add chords_

78. **Extension** — A note stacked above the 7th of a seventh chord, carrying on in 3rds: the 9th, 11th and 13th. G9, for example, is G7 with the 9th added on top.
   *Why it works:* Stacking 3rds past the 7th gives the 9th, 11th and 13th, which are the 2nd, 4th and 6th an octave up, adding colour without changing the root.
   _match: extension, extensions_

79. **Dominant 7th chord** — A major triad plus a flat 7th (a whole step below the root an octave up), written simply as 7: G7, C7, D7. For open G7, play G and move the note on string 1 from fret 3 to fret 1.
   *Why it works:* Its major 3rd and flat 7th form a tense, clashing interval that pulls toward the chord a 5th lower, which is why blues and rock lean on it.
   _match: dominant 7th, dominant seventh, dominant 7ths, dominant sevenths_

80. **Open chord / open string** — An open string is played with no finger on any fret. An open chord sits near the nut and lets some open strings ring: E, A, D, G, C, Em and Am are the common ones.
   *Why it works:* Open strings ring longer and fuller than fretted ones, and open chords double the root and 5th across octaves, strengthening the consonant ring.
   _match: open chord, open chords, open string, open strings_

81. **Barre** — One finger, usually the first, pressed flat across several strings at the same fret, with the other fingers making a shape in front of it. A barre chord can move to any fret to change its name.
   *Why it works:* Each fret is a half step, so moving a barre shape up two frets raises the whole chord a whole step: one shape gives all twelve major chords.
   _match: barre, barres, barre chord, barre chords_

82. **Power chord** — Just two notes, the root and its 5th, on neighbouring low strings: first finger on the root, third finger two frets higher on the next string up. Written with a 5, as in A5. It has no 3rd, so it is neither major nor minor.
   *Why it works:* The 5th is the most consonant interval after the octave, so the pair sounds solid and stays clear even with heavy distortion, where a full chord turns muddy.
   _match: power chord, power chords_

83. **CAGED** — A system linking the five open chord shapes C, A, G, E and D. Each can be moved up the neck as a barre shape, so any chord can be played in five places, one shape joining the next.
   *Why it works:* The fretboard repeats every twelve frets, an octave, so the five shapes join end to end and cover the whole neck for any root.
   _match: caged, e-shape, a-shape, c-shape, g-shape, d-shape_

84. **Double-stop** — Two notes played together on two strings, usually neighbours (sometimes with one string skipped), picked or strummed. They are often taken from the chord being played, such as the top two strings of a barre shape.
   *Why it works:* Two notes make one interval, the core of every chord; 3rds and 6ths sound sweet and consonant, which is why players reach for them most.
   _match: double-stop, double-stops, double stop, double stops_

85. **Slash chord** — A chord written with a slash, as in G/B: play G, but with B as the lowest note. The letter after the slash says which note goes on the lowest string you play.
   *Why it works:* Changing the lowest note shifts the chord's weight without changing its name; G/B is simply G in first inversion, with the 3rd on the bottom.
   _match: slash chord, slash chords_

86. **Progression** — A series of chords played in order, often looped, such as G, C, D, G. Most songs are built from a few progressions repeated.
   *Why it works:* Chords that share notes or whose roots move by a 5th connect smoothly, and moving away from the home chord and back gives a sense of journey.
   _match: progression, progressions, chord progression, chord progressions_

87. **Roman numerals (I, IV, V…)** — Roman numerals count chords from the home note: I is the chord built on note 1, IV on note 4, V on note 5. Upper case means major and lower case means minor, as in ii, iii and vi.
   *Why it works:* Counting by scale degree shows a progression's shape in any key: I-IV-V is G-C-D in G and A-D-E in A, because the major scale's step pattern never changes.
   _match: numeral, numerals, roman numeral, roman numerals_

88. **Harmony** — Notes sounding at the same time, and the way chords follow one another. Strumming chords under a sung tune is harmony.
   *Why it works:* Notes whose frequencies line up in simple ratios, like an octave (2:1) or a 5th (3:2), share overtones and blend, which is why some combinations sound consonant.
   _match: harmony, harmonies_

89. **Melody** — A tune: single notes one after another, the part you would sing or hum. On guitar it is usually picked one note at a time, often on the higher strings.
   *Why it works:* A melody moves mostly by half and whole steps through the scale, and it feels settled when it lands on a note of the chord underneath.
   _match: melody, melodies, melodic_

90. **Resolve / resolution** — Moving from a restless sound to a settled one, usually by landing on the home chord or home note. You hear it as the end of a musical sentence.
   *Why it works:* Clashing intervals sound unstable, and the ear wants them to move by a half step or whole step to consonant ones; that move is resolution.
   _match: resolve, resolves, resolved, resolving, resolution, unresolved_

91. **Tension** — The restless, unfinished feeling of a chord or note that wants to move on, such as a V chord just before the home chord. Building and releasing tension keeps music moving forward.
   *Why it works:* Dissonant intervals, like the clash between the 3rd and 7th of a dominant 7th chord, create tension; resolving them to consonant ones releases it.
   _match: tension_

92. **Dominant (V)** — The chord built on the 5th note of the scale, labelled V: D in the key of G. It pulls strongly back to the home chord, I.
   *Why it works:* V contains the note a half step below the home note (the 7th degree), which leans up into it; adding the flat 7th makes the pull even stronger.
   _match: dominant_

93. **Secondary dominant** — A major or dominant 7th chord that acts as the V of a chord other than home. In G, A7 is the V of D (written V/V), so it pulls toward D rather than G.
   *Why it works:* It copies the pull of a V chord by adding a note from outside the key (often a sharp), so the target chord briefly sounds like a new home note.
   _match: secondary dominant, secondary dominants, v of v_

94. **Borrowed chord** — A chord taken from the parallel key, the minor key with the same home note (or the major, if you are in minor). In G major, C minor (iv) and F major (bVII) are borrowed from G minor.
   *Why it works:* Swapping a note of the major scale for its flat version, such as the 3rd, 6th or 7th, darkens the chord while the home note stays the same.
   _match: borrowed chord, borrowed chords, borrowing, borrow, borrows_

95. **Colour note** — A note that gives a scale or chord its own flavour, usually one fret above or below a note of the major scale: b3, b6, b7 or #4. In G, b7 is F, one fret below F#.
   *Why it works:* Each mode or borrowed chord differs from the major scale by one or two notes, and those half-step changes are what the ear hears as colour.
   _match: colour note, colour notes, color note, color notes_

96. **Voice leading** — Moving each note of one chord to the nearest note of the next, so the change sounds smooth. On guitar it often means choosing inversions so your fingers barely move.
   *Why it works:* Notes moving by a half step or whole step are easy for the ear to follow, so small moves join chords smoothly and shared notes can simply stay put.
   _match: voice leading, voice-leading_

97. **Harmonic rhythm** — How often the chords change: every four counts, twice as often, or only now and then. It is separate from how fast you strum.
   *Why it works:* Each chord change is a new event the ear tracks against the steady pulse, so faster changes raise energy and slower ones feel calmer.
   _match: harmonic rhythm_

98. **Modulation (key change)** — Changing key partway through a song, so a new home note takes over. A common pop move shifts everything up one or two frets for the last section.
   *Why it works:* A key is a scale built on a home note; moving to a new home changes which notes sound stable and which sound restless, so the music feels lifted.
   _match: modulation, modulate, modulates, key change, key changes_

99. **Pivot chord** — A chord that belongs to both the old key and the new one, used as the hinge for a key change. The keys of G and D share G, D, Em and Bm.
   *Why it works:* Because the pivot fits both keys, the ear accepts it in the old key, then hears it pointing to the new home, so the change sounds smooth rather than sudden.
   _match: pivot chord, pivot chords, pivot-chord_

100. **Beat** — The steady pulse you tap your foot to. Count it out loud as 1 2 3 4: each number is one beat, and every strum or picked note is timed against it.
   *Why it works:* Pulse is the floor of all rhythm: every longer or shorter sound is measured in beats, the way pitch is measured in frets.
   _match: beat, beats_

101. **Bar** — A group of beats, usually four, counted 1 2 3 4 and then back to 1. Also called a measure. "One chord per bar" means change chord each time you get back to 1.
   *Why it works:* Bars give the pulse a shape: beat 1 feels strongest, so chord changes and new ideas usually land there, and songs are built in 4-bar and 8-bar blocks.
   _match: bar, bars_

102. **Tempo (bpm)** — Tempo is how fast the beats go, measured in bpm (beats per minute): 60 bpm is one beat each second. The app raises the tempo by 5 bpm after each clean pass and drops it back 5 after two misses.
   *Why it works:* Tempo changes how fast the pulse ticks, not where things land in the bar, so a pattern learned slowly and cleanly stays correct as you speed it up.
   _match: bpm, tempo_

103. **Downbeat** — Beat 1 of a bar: the first count, where you land each time you count 1 2 3 4 and start again. More loosely, any beat you count with a number rather than an "&".
   *Why it works:* The downbeat is the strongest point of the bar, so landing a chord or root there sounds settled, and moving away from it creates pull.
   _match: downbeat, downbeats_

104. **Subdivision (8ths, 16ths)** — Splitting each beat into smaller equal parts. Two per beat are 8ths, counted "1 & 2 & 3 & 4 &". Four per beat are 16ths, counted "1 e & a 2 e & a". Your strumming hand moves down on the numbers and up on the "&".
   *Why it works:* Subdivision is the ruler inside the beat: counting the small parts keeps every strum evenly spaced, and it is where all rhythm patterns are placed.
   _match: subdivision, subdivisions, 8ths, 16ths, 8th, 16th, eighth, eighths, sixteenth, sixteenths_

105. **Offbeat / the "and"** — The points halfway between the beats: the "&" when you count "1 & 2 & 3 & 4 &". On guitar the offbeats usually fall on the up-strums.
   *Why it works:* Sounds on the offbeat lean against the pulse instead of sitting on it, which creates lift and push; reggae and ska guitar live there.
   _match: offbeat, offbeats, off-beat_

106. **Triplet** — Three equal parts squeezed into one beat instead of two, counted "1 trip-let 2 trip-let". It gives a rolling, three-step feel compared with plain 8ths.
   *Why it works:* A beat can divide into two or three; three-way division drives the long-short blues feel and 6/8 time, so learn to hear both kinds of subdivision.
   _match: triplet, triplets_

107. **Shuffle / swing** — A feel where each pair of 8ths is played long-short instead of even, using the first and last parts of a triplet: "1-a-lah, 2-a-lah". Blues and jazz are usually played this way.
   *Why it works:* Swing keeps the beat and bar exactly the same but changes the subdivision from two even parts to a long-short triplet split, which is why it feels loping.
   _match: shuffle, shuffles, swing, swings, swung_

108. **Syncopation** — Putting strong sounds where the listener does not expect them: on an offbeat, or just before a beat, instead of on the beat itself.
   *Why it works:* Our ears expect weight on the strong beats; syncopation breaks that pattern on purpose, creating a small surprise that makes rhythm feel alive.
   _match: syncopation, syncopated_

109. **Anticipation** — Playing a chord change a little early, usually on the "&" of beat 4, so it arrives before beat 1 of the next bar instead of on it.
   *Why it works:* It is a common kind of syncopation: the change lands on an offbeat, so the downbeat it replaces feels pushed forward.
   _match: anticipation, anticipated, anticipations, anticipate_

110. **Backbeat** — Stress on beats 2 and 4 of a four-beat bar, where the drummer usually hits the snare. On guitar it is often a muted, percussive strum or a harder strum on 2 and 4.
   *Why it works:* Classical music tends to stress beats 1 and 3; stressing 2 and 4 is a built-in syncopation, and it is the engine of most rock, pop and soul.
   _match: backbeat, backbeats_

111. **Accent** — Making one sound louder or sharper than the ones around it, such as a harder strum on certain counts, so it stands out.
   *Why it works:* Accents decide which parts of the subdivision you hear as important; moving them is how a fixed strumming motion becomes a recognisable rhythm.
   _match: accent, accents, accented_

112. **Groove** — The overall rhythmic feel of a part: the tempo, which beats are accented, and whether it is straight or swung, settling into a pattern that makes people want to move.
   *Why it works:* Groove is beat, subdivision and accent locked together and repeated, steady enough to predict and varied enough to be interesting.
   _match: groove, grooves_

113. **Vamp** — A short chord pattern repeated over and over, often a single chord held for many bars. Funk lives on one-chord vamps: the band sits on one chord and the groove does the work.
   *Why it works:* With the harmony standing still, your ear moves to rhythm and tone, so small changes in timing and muting become the music.
   _match: one-chord vamp, vamp, vamps_

114. **Dynamics** — How loud or soft the music is, and how that changes over time. On guitar: how hard you strum or pick, and how many strings ring.
   *Why it works:* Volume changes shape tension and release just as harmony does; a quiet section makes the loud one feel bigger.
   _match: dynamics, dynamic_

115. **Stop-time** — The band plays one short, sharp hit, usually on beat 1, then goes silent for the rest of the bar (or longer) while the singer or soloist carries on, before everyone comes back in.
   *Why it works:* The pulse keeps going in everyone's head during the silence; stop-time shows how strong the beat is by taking the sound away from it.
   _match: stop-time, stop time_

116. **12-bar blues** — The classic blues layout over 12 bars: four of I, two of IV, two of I, then V, IV, I, V. Usually played as dominant 7th chords with a long-short, swinging strum; variants move to IV in bar 2 or shorten it to 8 bars.
   *Why it works:* I, IV and V are the home chord and its two closest neighbours, a 5th below and a 5th above, so the pattern feels like leaving home and returning.
   _match: 12-bar blues, 12-bar, twelve-bar blues, twelve-bar_

117. **Quick change** — A 12-bar blues that moves to the IV chord in bar 2 and back to I in bar 3, instead of staying on I for the first four bars. In G: G7, C7, G7, G7.
   *Why it works:* In G, the IV chord C7 contains B flat, the flat 3rd of G and a blue note, so the quick change brings that bluesy clash in early.
   _match: quick change, quick-change, quick to four_

118. **Turnaround** — The last two bars of a 12-bar blues, which head back to V so the form can start again. On guitar it is often a short lick walking down the higher strings onto V7.
   *Why it works:* Ending on V leaves the tension unresolved, so the ear expects the home chord next and the pattern rolls naturally into its next pass.
   _match: turnaround, turnarounds_

119. **Palm muting** — Resting the edge of your picking hand lightly on the strings right next to the bridge while you play, so they give a short, chunky thud instead of ringing.
   *Why it works:* Muting cuts the ringing so each strum becomes short, which lets accents and rests stand out clearly against the beat.
   _match: palm muting, palm mute, palm-muted, palm muted, palm mutes_

120. **Boogie (5–6 riff)** — A rolling two-note riff on the low strings: a power chord (root + 5th), then the little finger reaches two frets further to make root + 6th, back and forth on every beat with a shuffle feel. Guitarists took it from the left hand of boogie-woogie piano.
   *Why it works:* The root never moves, so the low end stays anchored, while the 5th-to-6th rocking adds motion; swung, it drives a 12-bar blues like a train.
   _match: boogie, boogies, root-5/root-6_

121. **Chuck** — A percussive strum where the fretting hand relaxes just enough to stop the strings ringing, so you hear a short "chk" instead of the chord. Shown as × in strumming patterns.
   *Why it works:* A chuck turns the guitar into a drum: placed on beats 2 and 4 it plays the backbeat, while the other strums carry the chord.
   _match: chuck, chucks_

122. **Ghost strum** — A strum where your hand keeps moving down and up in time but barely touches the strings, or misses them, so it is nearly silent.
   *Why it works:* Ghost strums keep the hand marking every subdivision, so the strums you do hear land exactly in time.
   _match: ghost strum, ghost strums, ghost, ghosts_

123. **Hammer-on** — Pick a note, then bring another finger down hard onto a higher fret of the same string so a second note sounds without picking again.
   *Why it works:* The string is already vibrating; shortening it with a new fret raises the pitch, which is how two notes come from one pick stroke.
   _match: hammer-on, hammer-ons, hammer on, hammered_

124. **Pull-off** — The reverse of a hammer-on: with two fingers on the same string, pick the higher note, then flick the top finger off sideways so the lower note sounds without picking again.
   *Why it works:* Removing a finger lengthens the vibrating string, so the pitch drops; the flick re-plucks the string to keep it sounding.
   _match: pull-off, pull-offs, pull off, pulled off_

125. **Slide** — Play a note, then keep pressing while you move the same finger along the string to another fret, so the sound glides to the new note without picking again.
   *Why it works:* Moving the finger changes the length of string that vibrates, so the pitch glides through every half step between the two frets.
   _match: slide, slides, sliding, slid_

126. **p-i-m-a** — Letters for the picking-hand fingers, from their Spanish names: p = pulgar (thumb), i = indice (index), m = medio (middle), a = anular (ring). Usually the thumb plays the low strings and i, m, a one higher string each.
   *Why it works:* Giving each finger its own string lets you play the notes of a chord separately and at the same time, like a small band in one hand.
   _match: p-i-m-a, pima, p-i-m_

127. **Pinch** — Plucking a low string with the thumb and a higher string with a finger at the same moment, as if pinching the strings together.
   *Why it works:* A pinch sounds a low and a high note together, giving the root underneath and the melody on top in one motion.
   _match: pinch, pinches, pinched_

128. **Arpeggio** — The notes of a chord played one after another instead of all at once, for example picking the strings of a chord shape one at a time.
   *Why it works:* An arpeggio spells the chord out (root, 3rd, 5th) in time, so you hear each note and the harmony at once.
   _match: arpeggio, arpeggios, arpeggiated, arpeggiate_

129. **Alternate bass / alternating thumb** — The thumb switches back and forth between two low strings on the beats, usually the root and another note of the chord, while the fingers pick higher strings in between.
   *Why it works:* Alternating root and 5th down low marks every beat, so a single guitar covers both the pulse and the harmony.
   _match: alternate bass, alternating bass, alternating thumb_

130. **Travis picking** — A fingerstyle pattern named after Merle Travis: the thumb alternates between two low strings on every beat while the fingers pick higher strings on the "&" between them.
   *Why it works:* The steady thumb is the pulse and the fingers play the offbeats, so the pattern is a built-in syncopation.
   _match: travis, travis picking, travis-picking, travis pattern_

131. **Bass and treble** — Bass means the low notes, on the thick strings (6, 5 and 4); treble means the high notes, on the thin strings (3, 2 and 1).
   *Why it works:* The lowest note decides how a chord sounds most strongly, so the bass usually plays the root while the treble carries the melody and colour.
   _match: bass, treble, bass note, bass notes_

132. **Verse** — The part of a song that tells the story: the music repeats each time but the words change. Verses usually lead into the main section that repeats with the same words.
   *Why it works:* Verses usually sit lower and calmer in energy so the next section can lift above them; the contrast is what makes the song feel like it goes somewhere.
   _match: verse, verses_

133. **Chorus** — The part of a song that comes back with the same words each time, usually carrying the title and the most memorable melody. It is often the loudest, highest part.
   *Why it works:* The chorus releases the tension built in the verse, often by landing on the home chord and raising the energy.
   _match: chorus, choruses_

134. **Pre-chorus** — A short section between the verse and the chorus that builds anticipation, often with new chords, rising energy, and stopping just before the chorus arrives.
   *Why it works:* The pre-chorus builds tension, often ending on the dominant or an unresolved chord, so the chorus feels like a release when it lands.
   _match: pre-chorus, pre-choruses, prechorus_

135. **Phrase** — A short musical idea with a beginning, middle and end, like a sentence in speech, usually 2 or 4 bars long and often ending with a breath or pause.
   *Why it works:* Phrases end on a goal, often a resolution to the home note or chord, which is why music feels like it is speaking in sentences.
   _match: phrase, phrases, phrasing_

136. **Register** — How high or low a passage sits overall: the same melody can be played in a low, middle or high register, for example lower on the neck or an octave up.
   *Why it works:* Moving to a higher register raises energy and brightness without changing the notes, which is why choruses often sit higher than verses.
   _match: register, registers_

137. **Prosody** — When the music fits what the words mean: the melody, rhythm, chords and phrasing all support the feeling of the lyric. Stressed syllables fall on strong beats.
   *Why it works:* Strong beats, rising melody, and resolution all carry feeling on their own; prosody lines those feelings up with the meaning of the words.
   _match: prosody_
