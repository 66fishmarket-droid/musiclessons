Accents and Palm Muting in G

Controlling which strings ring and which stay quiet lets you shape the groove so your vocal sits on top instead of fighting the guitar.

Theory card

The Circle of Fifths shows that G major has one sharp, F#, because it sits one step clockwise from C on the circle. The progression G–C–G–D moves I–IV–I–V, with D as the dominant chord a perfect fifth above the tonic. This dominant-to-tonic pull is built into the geometry of the circle itself.

warmup

  1. Hum a comfortable pitch on 'mm' for thirty seconds, then lip-trill up and down gently.
  2. Play the G major scale at the third position, saying each degree aloud: one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, one.
  3. Strum a single G chord, letting the bass ring while you lightly touch the higher strings to feel a manual mute.

Warming up the voice and fingers together sets your internal tempo and wakes up string-dampening reflexes.

new_skill

  1. Hear: Clap the boom-chick grid—bass on 1 and 3, chick on 2 and 4—at 46 bpm, exaggerating the accent.
  2. Learn: Rest your palm edge on the strings near the bridge, strike the low E or A for the boom, then release pressure slightly so the chick is muted but rhythmic.
  3. Play: Run the pattern on G at 46 bpm, accenting beats 1 and 3, palm-muting 2 and 4; keep the hand moving in straight eighths even when notes are silent.
  4. Climb the tempo ladder: 46, 54, 62, 70 bpm, two minutes each, never losing the accent shape.

Target: Play boom-chick with accents on 1 and 3 and palm-muted chicks on 2 and 4 at 70 bpm

Tip: Common mistake: muting so hard the chicks disappear entirely. Fix: lighten palm pressure until you hear a dry, percussive thud with pitch.

Accents and palm muting create dynamic breathing room in a folk groove; today you learn to make the guitar speak in sentences, not one long drone.

reset

  1. Put the guitar down for 30 seconds, shake out both hands, and picture your palm floating just above the saddle like a hover-bridge.

apply

  1. Set a loop at 70 bpm and play the G–C–G–D progression with boom-chick, singing a simple 'la' melody over it.
  2. On each chord change, move the bass note to the root—low E for G and D, A string for C—while keeping the mute hand steady.
  3. Add one fill only every four bars: a quick hammer-on from the second to third fret of the low E during a G measure.
  4. Sing a full verse of your own lyrics or 'la-la' syllables, letting your vocal sit in the spaces the muted strings leave.

Tip: If the vocal drops out when the chord changes, keep the strum hand moving and simplify to bass-only on the first beat of each new chord.

Applying the skill to a real progression with singing tests whether your groove survives the demands of performance.

create

  1. Use the create prompt: write one four-line verse in G where the third line lands on D and asks a question the fourth line answers on G.
  2. Play boom-chick under your new verse twice through, palm-muting every backbeat.
  3. Record a voice memo of just this section, listening for whether the accents push the lyric forward.

Writing into the progression cements the physical pattern into musical memory.

record

  1. Record one full pass of your new verse with boom-chick accompaniment at 70 bpm.
  2. Play it back and count how many bars had clean palm mutes versus accidental ringing; note one fix for tomorrow.

Recording reveals whether your accents and mutes are consistent enough to support a vocal in a real mix.

Create: Write one four-line verse in G where the third line lands on the D chord and lyrically asks a question that the fourth line answers back on G.

Songs

  • Wonderwall (Oasis, capo 2): The accented strum pattern with palm-muted verses shows how dynamic contrast builds a hook.
  • Blackbird (The Beatles, capo 0): The alternating bass-chick texture is a fingerstyle cousin to boom-chick, teaching note separation.
  • Dust in the Wind (Kansas, capo 0): The Travis-picked pattern uses bass accents on the beat with muted or plucked inner strings between.

Day 1: Notes on Strings 6 & 5

Knowing the notes on your two lowest strings unlocks every chord shape and power-chord root on the fretboard, so you stop guessing where to move.

Theory card

Scale degrees number the notes of any key 1 through 7. In G major, G is 1, A is 2, B is 3, and so on up to F# as 7. Because the pattern stays fixed, calling notes by degree lets you transpose instantly—degree 4 in G is C, and degree 4 in D is G. The low E and A strings carry the roots of most chords you play, so naming their notes by degree builds a mental map that works in every key.

warmup

  1. Hum a gentle scale up and down on 'oo,' then lip-trill softly.
  2. Play the G major scale on strings 6 and 5 in one position, saying each degree aloud: 1 on the 3rd fret E string, 2 on the 5th fret, and so on.

retest

  1. Set a metronome to 75 bpm.
  2. Loop Dsus4–D–Dsus2 with clean hammer-ons and pull-offs for two minutes, keeping the pulse steady.

Target: Play Dsus4–D–Dsus2 hammer-ons cleanly at 75 bpm.

Tip: If your hammer-ons buzz, press closer to the fret wire and attack with the fingertip, not the pad.

Retesting locks in the sus vocabulary you built earlier so it stays available while you add new fretboard knowledge.

new_skill

  1. Hear: Play the open 6th string and say 'E, that's degree 6 in G.' Play the 3rd fret and say 'G, degree 1.' Play the 5th fret and say 'A, degree 2.' Do the same on the 5th string: open 'A, degree 2,' 2nd fret 'B, degree 3,' 3rd fret 'C, degree 4,' 5th fret 'D, degree 5.'
  2. Learn: Without the guitar, quiz yourself—say the note name and degree for frets 0, 3, 5, 7 on both strings until you have zero hesitation.
  3. Play: At a slow tempo, play each note and name it aloud in under one second, three clean times through.

Target: Name any note on strings 6 and 5 instantly, three clean reps.

Tip: Don't rush to speed; accuracy builds speed. If you hesitate, return to the same fret until it feels automatic.

These two strings hold the roots of almost every chord you strum; owning them ends the 'hunt and peck' approach to the neck.

  2. Sing the root of each chord in the I–IV–V as you strum, naming the interval you hear from the previous root.
  3. Move the same rhythm pattern to C and D, keeping the subdivisions even and the accents sharp.

Tip: If your sixteenths blur, slow until each down-up-down-up is identical in volume.

Locked rhythms and interval awareness are the glue that makes new fretboard knowledge musical rather than mechanical.

  3. Sing a simple melody over the progression while maintaining the boom-chick feel, adding one Dsus4–D fill in the final four bars.

Applying fretboard knowledge inside a real groove forces you to find roots under pressure, which is exactly what performing demands.

p-i-m-a and pinches in D

Fingerstyle lets you be your own accompanist: bass, harmony, and melody all come from your hands while you sing. The pinch is the gateway to independent thumb control.

Theory card

The Circle of Fifths shows D major sitting two steps clockwise from C, with two sharps (F# and C#). Its closest neighbors are G (one flat, counter-clockwise) and A (three sharps, clockwise). This is why D, G, and A feel so natural together in folk music—they are family members on the circle.

warmup

  1. Hum a descending fifth from D down to G, then lip-trill back up.
  2. Play the D major scale in open position, naming degrees aloud: "one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, one."
  3. Sing the root D on beat 1 while pinching the open D string and high E string together gently.

Tip: Keep your thumb vertical and plucking near the soundhole; your fingers should stay over strings 1-3.

Warming up voice and fingers together builds the coordination you'll need for singing while fingerpicking.

new_skill

  1. Listen: tap your foot in four and say "bass-fingers-bass-fingers" to feel the boom-chick groove.
  2. Place thumb on string 4 (D), index on 3, middle on 2, ring on 1. Pinch thumb and fingers together on beat 1, then pluck fingers alone on beat 3.
  3. Loop the pinch pattern on D at 39 bpm: pinch on 1, rest 2, fingers on 3, rest 4. Keep your thumb moving steady.
  4. Increase to 49 bpm, then 60 bpm, only moving up when the pinch feels clean and thumb control stays independent.

Target: Play the p-i-m-a pinch pattern cleanly starting at 39 bpm and building to 60 bpm.

Tip: If your fingers rush ahead of your thumb, slow down and tap your foot louder—thumb follows the foot, fingers follow the thumb.

Hearing the groove first, then isolating the thumb-finger independence, then playing at tempo creates lasting muscle memory.

reset
