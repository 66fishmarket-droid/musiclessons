3. Play G again as the melody note. Sing a 3rd above it, then a 6th above it, back to back, without checking each on the guitar first.
4. Rate yourself 1–5 on how in-tune each harmony note felt against the melody note.

*Listen for:* The harmony note sitting clearly above the melody note, never unison or below it.

### Colour notes (L4) — card: scale · major keys only

1. Turn on the drone. b7 sits one fret below F#; b3 sits one fret below B. Play and sing each against the drone.
2. b6 sits one fret below E; #4 sits one fret above C. Play and sing those two the same way.
3. Pick any two colour notes and sing them back to back, without replaying them on the guitar first.
4. Rate yourself 1–5 on how confidently you found each colour note by ear.

*Listen for:* Each colour note sitting clearly darker or brighter than its nearby scale degree, not identical to it.

### Borrowed chords by ear (L4) — card: scale · major keys only

1. Play {G} {C} {D} {G} straight through once so the plain, diatonic version is fresh in your ear.
2. iv and bVI borrow a note from outside the key: one fret below E (b6); bVII borrows one fret below F# (b7). Sing each borrowed note over the drone.
3. Play {G} {C} {D} {G} again. On one bar, swap the chord for iv, bVI or bVII in your head and sing the changed note before you hear it played.
4. Rate yourself 1–5 on how clearly the borrowed chord stood out.

*Listen for:* The borrowed chord sounding darker or further from home than the diatonic chords around it.

### Harmony while strumming (L5) — card: scale

1. Strum {G} {C} {D} {G} at a steady pace until the chord changes need no attention at all.
2. Pick a harmony line a 3rd or 6th above the melody, using G, B and E as your reference points in today's key.
3. Strum {G} {C} {D} {G} again, singing that harmony line the whole way through. If the hands falter, hum it instead of singing words.
4. Rate yourself 1–5 on how steady the strumming stayed while you sang.

*Listen for:* The strum pattern completely unaffected by the voice coming in.

### Transcribe by ear (L5) — card: scale

1. Play G, A, B, C, D, E and F# on the guitar or hum them, so every degree is fresh before you start.
2. Play the first chord of your chosen song. Sing its root, then match that pitch to a degree on the card.
3. Write down the numeral for that degree, then repeat for every new chord until the progression loops.
4. Rate yourself 1–5 on how many chords you placed correctly.

*Listen for:* Each root landing cleanly on one scale degree, not hovering between two.


## songwriting

### Core loops (L1) — card: chords

1. Play {G} {C} {D} {G}, one bar each, until it loops without a gap.
2. Now start the same loop from the second chord, then from the third. Each start gives the same chords a different mood.
3. Hum over your favourite start and note which one felt most like a chorus, the song's most repeated section.
4. Rate yourself 1–5 on how smooth the loop felt.

*Listen for:* The loop landing back on its first chord without a hiccup.

### Object writing and prosody (L1) — card: none

1. Set a 5-minute timer. Pick one thing nearby and write only what your senses notice about it — sight, sound, smell, touch, taste — no crossing out.
2. A stable line reads like a finished sentence; an unstable line trails off, like a question. Mark each line you wrote S or U.
3. Write one more line that flips your best line: stable if it was unstable, or the reverse.
4. Rate yourself 1–5 on how much of it was senses, not opinions.

*Listen for:* Concrete, sensory words rather than vague feelings or judgments.

### Verse/chorus contrast (L2) — card: chords

1. Play {G} {C} {D} {G}, one chord per bar, starting on {G}: that's your verse, the section that tells the story between choruses.
2. For a chorus, the song's most repeated section, change two levers: start on a chord other than {G}, and double the harmonic rhythm, how often the chord changes.
3. Hum a line over each version, singing the chorus higher in your range than the verse: that's a third lever, register.
4. Rate yourself 1–5 on how different the two sections felt.

*Listen for:* A clearly different starting point, speed of change and register between the two.

### Melody skeleton (L2) — card: chords

1. Play {G} {C} {D} {G}, one chord per bar. A melody skeleton is the handful of long notes a tune is built from before it gets decorated.
2. Pick one note per bar to be your skeleton, favouring the stable notes G, B and D: four notes total, one per chord.
3. Sing just those four skeleton notes, holding each one for its whole bar.
4. Now decorate: add one short extra note before or after each skeleton note, then sing the decorated version.
5. Rate yourself 1–5 on how clearly the skeleton still shows through the decoration.

*Listen for:* The held skeleton note still landing on the beat once the decoration is added around it.

### Pre-chorus tension (L3) — card: chords

1. A pre-chorus is a short section between verse and chorus whose job is to raise tension before the chorus lands.
2. Play {G} {C} {D} {G} for two bars, then land the last bar on the chord built on D (or, for a softer pull, C) instead of back on {G}.
3. Speed up on the way there: change chords twice as often in that last bar as you did before.
4. Hum a rising line over it, ending unresolved on D itself.
5. Rate yourself 1–5 on how strongly it pulled you toward the next section.

*Listen for:* The last chord staying unresolved instead of settling home, so you want the next section to arrive.

### Borrowed colour (L3) — card: chords · major keys only

1. Borrowing means dropping one chord from the minor version of your key into an otherwise major progression, for a darker colour just for a moment.
2. Play {G} {C} {D} {G}. Then, in place of one bar, play a major chord built a whole step below G — that borrowed chord is called bVII.
3. Try it again, this time playing a minor chord built on C in place of one bar of {G} {C} {D} {G} — that borrowed chord is called iv.
4. Rate yourself 1–5 on how clearly each borrowed chord changed the mood before it resolved back to {G}.

*Listen for:* A sudden, deliberate darkening of colour that still resolves cleanly back to the home chord.

### Bridges designed backwards (L4) — card: chords

1. A bridge is a section, used once, that breaks the verse/chorus pattern to set up the final chorus.
2. Decide your chorus starts on {G}. Work backwards: your bridge must end on the chord built on D, so {G} lands like the answer.
3. Start the bridge on a chord you have not used to open the verse or chorus, then find your own path from there to D.
4. Play the bridge straight into {G} {C} {D} {G} and listen for the chorus landing like a relief.
5. Rate yourself 1–5 on how clearly the bridge set up that landing.

*Listen for:* The bridge ending on tension, then the chorus arriving like a resolved answer.

### Secondary dominants (L4) — card: chords · major keys only

1. A secondary dominant is a chord borrowed for one bar that pulls hard toward a chord other than home, the way a V chord normally pulls toward I.
2. Play {G} {C} {D} {G}. Right before the chord built on D, insert a major chord built on A — that extra pull toward D is called V of V.
3. Now pull toward E instead: insert a major chord built on B right before it — that is V of vi.
4. Play {G} {C} {D} {G} once more, treating E as home — that's the relative minor — then shift back to G as home, the relative major.
5. Rate yourself 1–5 on how clearly each move pulled toward its target.

*Listen for:* Extra pull into the target chord, sharper than the plain diatonic move would give.

### Key changes (L5) — card: chords

1. A modulation is a permanent change of home note partway through a song, used only when the lyric's energy earns it, not as decoration.
2. Try a truck-driver modulation: play {G} {C} {D} {G}, then play the same shapes one fret higher — the whole song stepping up is the classic final-chorus lift.
3. Now try a pivot-chord modulation: end on the chord built on C, a chord that exists in both keys, then treat it as the new home and resolve into it.
4. Say in one sentence why this moment in your song would earn a key change — if you can't, skip it.
5. Rate yourself 1–5 on how smooth (pivot) or how earned (truck-driver) the shift felt.

*Listen for:* A shift that feels inevitable, not bolted on — earned by the lyric, not just louder.

### Style transplant (L5) — card: chords

1. A style transplant takes a song you already have and moves it into another style by changing one lever at a time, not everything at once.
2. Play {G} {C} {D} {G} in today's groove, then play the same {G} {C} {D} {G} with a different groove — swap straight time for a shuffle, or the reverse.
3. Now change one more lever: the harmonic rhythm (how often chords change), or borrow one chord from the opposite mode — major borrows from minor, minor from major.
4. Hum your melody over the new version and notice which lever changed the mood the most.
5. Rate yourself 1–5 on how recognisable the song still was after the change.

*Listen for:* The same song, one clearly different lever at a time, still recognisable underneath.
